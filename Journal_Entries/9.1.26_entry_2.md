# Chapter 1: The Psychopathy of everyday things

### General Summary
This chapter really works hard to bring together several different perspectives of Design to get the gears going. One of the subjects that's brought up is the idea that if you need to very clearly label how not to use something- that's an example of bad design. The Norman door is specifically brought up in the text, but you also see it elsewhere, in restaurants, or in general buildings where there's a handle that implies that you twist and pull but instead you end up having to twist and push. In the end, if you want to have good design, it seems as if Don Norman argues that it should never be noticed at all.

![handle vs push bar](https://www.autodoorandhardware.com/v/vspfiles/assets/images/5F995421-E805-43CD-ADA0-B13521EF4A5C.jpeg)

Don Norman also breaks down different types of design, including interaction design industrial design and experience design all of which at its core fall under human centered design- but he also touches on the concept of engineering arguing that engineering takes a machine, focused, and people second approach, even though it should be the other way around people are going to make mistakes therefore, the machines that are engineered should account for that, and be laid out to prevent those issues.

Norman also mentioned in the previous versions of the book he included and coined the term of affordances- which are the physical properties of an object and the ways it could possibly be used, but as a result of the coining of this term, he started seeing it being used significantly more widely and more incorrectly. Which thus brings us to the need to introduce the term of a signifier which shows a specific way that an object can be used. After all- Communication is the key to good design and when communication falls apart, that is when the design falls apart as well, and where you begin to see failure.

Part of that communication is within mapping which lays out pieces of a design and show how they are connected so that the user can understand it. A key part of that is that the map doesn't actually have to be factually, correct. So long as the mapping is intuitive and the user can understand how to interact and use those controls- then the mapping works.

In order for users to know that that mapping works, though it also needs to have feedback. And feedback is tricky because it has to be timely and easy to understand.

### General Thoughts
This book was actually recommended to me by my sister almost 3 years ago, and I just never got around to sitting down and picking it up. So this has been good motivation for me to actually read it and get back to my sister with some of my thoughts on this.

When Norman brought up the three disciplines of design that make up human center design, I had a slight chuckle to myself, mostly because I am now currently getting formal education in all three facets of human centered design now. I focused in industrial design for my bachelors. My apprenticeship and my associates were in user experience design and interaction design. And now I am working towards my masters in interaction designed and interactive art. One of the things that we've spoken about in a different class, though is the idea of not using a user centered approach to design because while the user may be the primary person in contact with your design realistically it's going to affect several other things as well.

I imagine the Norman door is going to be brought up a lot in class already, but for my two cents on that-there was a period of time when I first moved to California, where I just could not open any of the doors in any of the offices on campus to the point where my coach had someone try and stick with me in case I ever locked myself into a room or just could not figure out how to enter a room.
